Digital Literacy Quick-Start Eight short walkthroughs for the online steps that benefits, housing, court, and job applications now require

Almost every door now has a screen in front of it. Applying for food benefits, getting a court date, keeping a housing caseworker updated, applying for work — all of it assumes you have an email address and know how to attach a file. Nobody is born knowing that, and nobody should have to pretend they do. Each section below is one task, in plain words, in the order you would actually do it. Start anywhere. Skip anything.

Nothing here leaves this page. This tool has no account, no sign-up, and no text boxes to type your information into. If you tick a box to mark a step done, that tick is stored only in this browser on this device so the page remembers where you were. On a library or shelter computer, that memory is wiped when the computer resets — that is normal, and it is the safe behavior. Nothing is ever sent to us.
There is no test and no score. There is no wrong order and no such thing as being behind. If a step already makes sense, skip it. If a step does not work the way it is written here, that is the software changing, not you doing it wrong — the buttons move around all the time.
0 of 8 marked done

Marking things done is only for your own place-keeping. It changes nothing else.

An email address is free, takes about ten minutes, and does not require an address, a phone, or an ID. Gmail (from Google) and Outlook (from Microsoft) are the two most widely accepted. Either is fine. The library computers listed further down have both.

  1. Open the internet browser on the computer or phone. Type gmail.com in the bar at the very top and press Enter.
  2. Choose Create account, then For my personal use.
  3. Type a first and last name. It does not have to be formal, but if you will use this for job or benefit applications, your real name is easier for a caseworker to match to your file.
  4. Pick a username. This becomes the part before the @. Plain is better than clever — a hiring manager and a case manager will both see it. If your first choice is taken, add numbers that are not your birth year.
  5. Make a password you can actually remember. Length beats complexity: three unrelated words strung together is both easier to remember and harder to break than a short jumble of symbols.
  6. Write the address and password down on paper and keep the paper somewhere separate from your phone and wallet. This is the single most common place people get stuck later.
  7. If it asks for a recovery phone number or a second email, you can usually choose Skip. If you have a working phone number, adding it makes it much easier to get back in if you forget the password.
If you do not have a phone number: a librarian can often help you finish the setup without one, and some free-phone programs are listed in step 8. If a site absolutely will not let you finish without a phone, stop and ask for help rather than using someone else's number — password resets will go to them.

Public computers are safe to use for private business as long as you close the door behind you. Three habits do almost all of the work.

  1. Say no to “Save password.” If a box slides in offering to remember your password, choose Never or Not now. On a shared machine, saving it hands your account to the next person.
  2. Use a private window when you can. In Chrome or Edge, press Ctrl + Shift + N; in Firefox, Ctrl + Shift + P. A private window forgets your sign-in when you close it. It does not hide you from the library or your employer — it just does not leave your account logged in.
  3. Sign out before you walk away. Click your initial or picture in the top-right corner and choose Sign out. Closing the tab alone does not always do it.
  4. Log off the computer itself at the end, and take anything you printed off the printer.
Good news about the county libraries: Maricopa County Library District computers delete your browsing history and any files you downloaded as soon as you log out, and their loaner Chromebooks get a full factory reset between borrowers. That is a backstop, not a substitute for signing out yourself.
If you are leaving an abusive situation: a shared or borrowed device may be monitored. Consider using a library computer instead of a phone that person has ever had access to, and see our safety resources. The Leave page link at the top right of this site jumps you to a weather page immediately.

An attachment is just a file riding along with the message. The paper clip icon is the whole trick.

  1. Open your email and choose Compose (Gmail) or New mail (Outlook).
  2. In the To line, type the address exactly — one wrong letter and it silently goes nowhere.
  3. In Subject, write what it is and who you are: Pay stubs — Maria Lopez, case 12345. Caseworkers handle hundreds of messages; a clear subject line gets yours found.
  4. Click the paper clip icon at the bottom of the message.
  5. A window opens showing your files. Click the file you want, then Open. If you just took a photo on a phone, look under Photos, Gallery, or Recent.
  6. Wait for the file name to appear inside the message with a small bar finishing. If you press Send before that bar finishes, the file does not go.
  7. Write one line in the message body so it does not look like spam, then Send.
  8. Check your Sent folder and open the message. If you can see the attachment there, they can see it too.
To attach more than one thing, click the paper clip again for each file — you do not have to send separate emails. If a file is refused for being too big, send the pages in two emails and say so in the subject: 1 of 2, 2 of 2.

A clear phone photo is accepted almost everywhere. Most rejections are about the photo, not the document.

  1. Put the paper on a flat, dark surface — a table, a car seat, a backpack. Dark behind white paper makes the edges obvious.
  2. Stand so your own shadow is not falling on the page. Daylight near a window or a doorway beats indoor light.
  3. Hold the phone directly above, parallel to the paper, and get all four corners inside the frame. A cut-off corner is the single most common reason a document gets rejected.
  4. Take the photo. Then open it and read the smallest print on it. If you cannot read it, they cannot either — take it again closer.
  5. One page per photo. Multi-page documents get one photo each, all attached to the same email.
  6. Attach it using step 3, or upload it using step 6.
If you need a real scan or a printed copy: every Maricopa County Library District branch has public scanners and printers. Printing runs $0.25 a page in black and white and $0.75 in color, paid with small bills, coins, or a card. Scanning to a USB drive or to your own email is generally the cheaper route.

Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet all work the same way: someone sends you a link, and clicking it at the right time is most of the job.

  1. Find the link before the day of. It is in the email or text as a long blue web address, usually with a meeting ID and sometimes a passcode. Write the ID and passcode on paper as a backup.
  2. Join five to ten minutes early. Click the link. If it asks whether to download an app or continue in the browser, continue in the browser — it is faster and works on library computers where you cannot install anything.
  3. The browser will ask for permission to use the camera and microphone. Choose Allow. Without this, you join and nobody can hear you.
  4. You may land in a waiting room until the host lets you in. That is normal. Do not leave and rejoin repeatedly.
  5. Find the microphone and camera icons at the bottom. A line through the icon means it is off. Stay muted until it is your turn to speak.
  6. If your internet is weak, turn your camera off and keep audio only. Sound is what matters.
Every one of these has a phone-only option. The invitation almost always includes a phone number and meeting ID you can dial from any phone, including a payphone or a borrowed one. For a court date especially, dialing in on time counts — not appearing does not. If you cannot find the dial-in number, call the office that sent the invitation and ask for it directly.
Somewhere quiet to take it: Maricopa County Library District branches let you reserve a free study room, which is a far better setting for a court appearance or an interview than a sidewalk. Ask at the desk or call (602) 652-3000.

Uploading is attaching, just on a website instead of in an email. The button is usually labelled Upload, Choose file, Browse, or Add document.

  1. Sign in to the portal and find the section named something like Documents, Verifications, or Upload proof.
  2. Some portals ask you to pick what the document is first — proof of income, proof of identity. Pick the closest match; if nothing fits, pick Other rather than giving up.
  3. Click Choose file, find your photo (usually under Photos, Downloads, or Recent), and select it.
  4. Look for the second button. Choosing the file is not the same as sending it. Almost every portal needs a separate Upload or Submit click afterward. This is the step people miss.
  5. Wait for a confirmation — a checkmark, a green bar, a reference number, or the file appearing in a list. Screenshot or write down that confirmation number.
  6. If the page freezes or times out, do not assume it failed and do not send it fifteen times. Sign back in and look at the document list to see whether it landed.
Keep a paper trail. Note the date, the time, and what you uploaded. If the agency later says it never arrived, that note is what gets the clock reset instead of your application closed. Our My Ladder tool has a place to keep that list.

You do not need to be technical to catch these. Nearly all of them break at least one of four rules.

  1. Real agencies do not demand payment in gift cards, cryptocurrency, or wire transfer. Not one. If anyone asks for a Green Dot card, an Apple or Walmart gift card number, or a Zelle transfer to hold a benefit, an apartment, or a job, it is a scam every time.
  2. Urgency is the tell. “Your case closes today,” “a warrant will be issued in one hour,” “three other people want this unit.” Rushing you past thinking is the entire technique. Real deadlines arrive in writing with time to act.
  3. Do not click the link in the message — go to the site yourself. If a text says your benefits card is locked, do not tap it. Type the agency's real address into the browser, or call the number on the back of the card or on your paperwork.
  4. Nobody legitimate needs your password. Not a caseworker, not tech support, not the housing office. A one-time code texted to you is also a password — if someone asks you to read them a code, hang up.
Rental listings are a common one here. If someone will not let you see a unit in person, claims to be out of state, or wants a deposit before you ever meet, walk away. Money sent this way is essentially never recovered.
If you already sent money or a code: call your bank or card issuer immediately and say the word “fraud,” then change the password on your email first, since email is the master key to everything else. You can report it to the Federal Trade Commission at reportfraud.ftc.gov. Being taken in by one of these says nothing about you — they are professionally built.

Everything in this section is either free or low-cost, and none of it requires an address of your own. Details were checked against each operator's own website on September 8, 2026 — call ahead anyway.

Maricopa County Library District — free computers, no library card needed

All 18 branches have public computers, printers, scanners, and Wi-Fi. You can use a computer with a library card or a free guest pass — you do not need to be a cardholder to get online. When other people are waiting, branches may cap internet sessions at 30 or 60 minutes a day. Machines run Windows 11 with Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Chrome, Edge, and Firefox, and your history and downloads are deleted when you log out. Free Wi-Fi (network MCLD Wireless Public, no password) reaches the parking lots while the branch is open. Printing is $0.25 a page black and white, $0.75 color. Questions: (602) 652-3000.

Maricopa County Library District — borrow a laptop and a hotspot for three weeks

“Chromebook To-Go” and “Hotspot To-Go” check out for three weeks, one of each at a time. You need a library card in good standing, you must be 18 or older, you pick the device up in person, and a librarian walks you through it and has you sign a borrowing agreement before you leave. Devices are factory-reset after every return, so your browsing does not follow you. A Maricopa County library card is free. Request or ask about availability at (602) 652-3000 or any branch.

Phoenix Public Library — 16 branches, Burton Barr Central, and a bookmobile

Burton Barr Central Library, 1221 N. Central Ave., Phoenix, AZ 85004 — (602) 262-4636. Library cards are free. The library's PHXWorks program bundles computer access with resume and interview help, and branches run free Basic Computer Skills classes with laptops provided. Ask at any desk which classes are running this month; schedules change.

PCs for People — refurbished computers and $15-a-month internet, by mail

A national nonprofit. There is no Phoenix storefront, so this is an online order shipped to you. You qualify if you are enrolled in an income-based assistance program (SNAP, Medicaid/AHCCCS, SSI, SSDI, LIHEAP, Section 8, Head Start, free or reduced lunch, Pell Grant, and others) or your household income is under 200% of the federal poverty guidelines. You will need a photo ID and a dated document proving that enrollment or income — handwritten notes are not accepted, and they do not ask for proof of citizenship. Order at pcsrefurbished.com; questions at (651) 354-2552.

Computers 2 Kids — low-cost refurbished computers, Phoenix

Sells refurbished computers to low-income individuals and households with students — roughly $80 for a desktop and $100 for a laptop, loaded with Windows, an office suite, antivirus, and a year of free tech support. Website: c2sdk.org. Prices and stock change; confirm before you travel.

Lifeline — a federal discount on phone or internet service

A monthly discount of $9.25 on phone or internet service ($34.25 on Tribal lands), one per household. You qualify through Medicaid/AHCCCS, SNAP, SSI, Federal Public Housing Assistance, or a Veterans Pension, or with household income at or below 135% of the federal poverty guidelines. Several carriers bundle it into a free basic phone plan. Start at lifelinesupport.org or call the Lifeline Support Center at 1-800-234-9473. Note that this is not the pandemic-era Affordable Connectivity Program, which ended — Lifeline is the one still running.

Free practice, at your own pace

GCFGlobal (edu.gcfglobal.org), a program of the Goodwill Community Foundation, has several hundred free step-by-step tutorials on email, computer basics, Word, and job searching. No registration is required and there is no cost at any point. It works on a library computer and picks up wherever the buttons have moved to.

One thing that changed: Arizona's statewide Connect Arizona Digital Navigator helpline — the free phone line for one-on-one tech help — was discontinued on April 1, 2025 when its federal funding ended. Some directories still list it. Your public library is now the working front door for hands-on help; Maricopa County Library District takes one-on-one Book-A-Librarian appointments at (602) 652-3000.

What to say when you ask for help

Asking is not a failure state, and library staff field this exact request constantly. A sentence that works at any desk:

“I need to send a document to my caseworker by email and I have not done that before. Can someone show me once?”

Naming the specific task gets you a person who will sit down with you, rather than a pointer toward a class next month. If the branch is slammed, ask whether they take one-on-one appointments — the county system does.
