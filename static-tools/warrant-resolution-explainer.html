Warrant Resolution Explainer What an outstanding warrant does — and doesn't — change, and the safest way to clear it

A warrant hanging over you is exhausting, and the worry is often bigger than the warrant itself. Most warrants people carry while unhoused are bench warrants — missed traffic court, an unpaid fine, a missed check-in — not the kind of thing that follows you into a shelter bed or a benefits application.       What an outstanding warrant does — and doesn't — change

      The fear is usually bigger than the reality. Here's what actually tends to happen, and what doesn't, based on how services in Maricopa County actually operate.

      Common questions

      Will a warrant stop me from getting into a shelter?

      Generally, no. Shelter intake staff at places like Central Arizona Shelter Services, the Human Services Campus, the Brian Garcia Welcome Center, and the Family Housing Hub are not law enforcement, and running warrant checks at intake is not standard practice in Maricopa County's shelter system — coordinated entry is built to get people inside, not to screen them out over legal history. That said, policies can vary by program and this page can't promise every provider's rule. If it's weighing on you, you can ask a shelter directly, or ask 2-1-1 to connect you somewhere first. What can affect a specific bed: safety concerns for other guests, active violent charges, or program-specific rules — not the mere existence of a bench warrant.

      Will it affect my SNAP, AHCCCS, TANF, or SSI benefits?

      For most people carrying a bench warrant — a missed traffic court date, an unpaid fine, a minor citation — the answer is no. Federal law only disqualifies someone from SNAP or TANF as a "fleeing felon," and that has a narrow legal meaning: it requires an outstanding felony warrant where law enforcement is actively trying to arrest you and has told the benefits agency they intend to. A warrant simply existing in a database is not enough to trigger it. If you're worried, don't assume you're disqualified and stop applying — call 2-1-1 or your DES caseworker and ask directly. AHCCCS (Arizona's Medicaid) does not use this rule at all.

      Can I still get a state ID or driver's license?

      Getting a standard Arizona ID card is not tied to warrant checks at MVD. A suspended or revoked driver's license is a separate issue from a warrant — it has its own reinstatement process, unrelated to whether you have an outstanding warrant on an unrelated matter. If you're working on getting ID sorted out, the ID Recovery Wizard maps the right order to get your documents back.

      What is Maricopa County Regional Homeless Court, and can it help me?

      The Maricopa County Regional Homeless Court (MCRHC) exists specifically to quash warrants and clear fines tied to minor misdemeanor cases for people working on ending their homelessness — converting what you'd owe into completed program hours instead. It is held the third Tuesday of every month at 204 S 12th Ave, Phoenix, AZ 85007. You don't apply directly: you enroll with a qualified case manager or program provider first (most require things like sobriety, housing, or treatment progress and some community service), and once you're making progress, your case manager applies to MCRHC on your behalf and appears with you. It cannot help with active felony warrants, charges with mandatory sentencing, or cases where a victim has invoked their rights. Ask any case manager you're working with whether they're a qualified MCRHC provider, or call the court's Information Center at 602-506-3204 to ask how to connect with one.

      What happens if I just ignore it?

      Warrants don't expire and don't go away on their own — they sit until an arrest happens, often at the worst possible moment: a traffic stop, a hospital visit, an unrelated police contact. Ignoring a missed court date can also add a new charge on top of the original one: Failure to Appear. For a misdemeanor case, that's a Class 2 misdemeanor (up to 4 months in jail, up to $750) the first time, rising to a Class 1 misdemeanor (up to 6 months, up to $2,500) if it happens again. For a felony case, Failure to Appear is itself a Class 5 felony (up to 1.5 years and a fine, though courts have discretion to reduce this with mitigating circumstances). Addressing it — even just a phone call to ask what's needed — almost always leaves you better off than waiting.
