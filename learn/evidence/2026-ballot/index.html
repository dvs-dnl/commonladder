How the scores work

Each item is scored from −2 to +2 on four ways policy changes homelessness. A positive score means the record points toward fewer people becoming or staying homeless.

DimensionScores up whenEvidence base
Housing supplyMore homes get built or rents fall: zoning reform, housing trust funds, LIHTC, action against rent-setting.Glynn & Fox (2019) found rising rents predict rising homelessness in large U.S. metros.
PreventionPeople keep the housing and income they have: eviction defense, rental assistance, Medicaid and behavioral health coverage.Collinson et al. (2024) found an eviction order raises the chance of using an emergency shelter the next year by 3.4 points, more than a 300% increase.
Services & fundingShelter, permanent supportive housing and Housing First programs gain money or protection.The Community Guide review (Peng et al., 2020) found Housing First improved housing stability by a median 41% over treatment-first programs.
EnforcementPolicy avoids moving or penalizing people without offering housing, and avoids new ID or paperwork barriers.Barocas et al. in JAMA (2023) projected that continual encampment displacement could account for 15–25% of deaths among unhoused people who use drugs over 10 years, across 23 cities.

Rules applied to every item

Record before statements. A dimension is scored from the record when one exists: votes, signed or vetoed bills, budgets and lawsuits, up to ±2. Campaign statements score a dimension only when there is no record, and at most ±1. Each dimension cell shows which kind it rests on.

Indirect effects. Mechanisms the research ties to homelessness only indirectly — voter ID, grocery taxes, school staffing, utility rates — are capped at ±1.

Overall score. The average of the scored dimensions. Candidates need at least two scored dimensions to get one; otherwise they show as not scored. A blank dimension means no record was found, not a zero.

Budgets. Voting for a budget counts +1 in each dimension it funds; leading a budget item counts up to +2. State budgets count for both the governor and the Senate President, who negotiate them. City budget votes count for every council member who cast one.

Score bands. +1.5 or higher: strongly reduces homelessness. +0.5 to +1.4: reduces. −0.4 to +0.4: mixed effect. −0.5 to −1.4: increases. −1.5 or lower: strongly increases.

Where the evidence is disputed

  • The same Housing First review found results unfavorable to Housing First on alcohol use, and its treatment-first comparisons rest on few studies.
  • Supporters of Prop 312 (2024), including the Goldwater Institute, say it compensates property owners and pushes cities to enforce existing laws. No outcome data on homelessness has been published either way. By September 2025, more than two dozen claims seeking about $128,000 had been filed, and none paid.
  • The displacement mortality figures come from a simulation model, not observed deaths.
  • Supporters of the 2025 reconciliation law say it targets fraud and ineligible enrollment rather than cutting Medicaid. The scores use CBO’s coverage estimates.
  • National research finds strict voter ID laws have little effect on overall turnout. No study isolates unhoused voters.

Statewide propositions

Eight measures are on the statewide ballot, all referred by the Legislature. Three affect homelessness. Props 141 (mileage tax ban), 142 (DEI and preferential treatment), 317 (drug cartels), 318 (student athletics) and 319 (photo enforcement) have no mechanism that touches housing, income, services or enforcement, and are not scored.

Prop 144 — Election requirements

Constitutional amendment (HCR 2001)

−1.0 Increases homelessness

Requires government-issued ID to vote in every election, including by mail. People without a stable address are less likely to hold current ID, and Arizona charges $12 for a state ID card for most adults.

Housing supplyPreventionServices & fundingEnforcement
———−1record
Data behind the score
  • Enforcement Ballot language: proof of identity limited to government-issued ID; the Legislature must fund implementation. Secretary of State ballot language, Aug 2026
  • Enforcement People experiencing homelessness face ID barriers: no address, lost or stolen documents, fees, transportation. GAO-24-105435, Feb 2024
  • Enforcement State ID card costs $12; fee waivers cover people 65+ and some veterans and SSI recipients, not most unhoused adults. ADOT MVD, accessed Oct 2026
  • Enforcement Texas's mail-ballot ID rule led to 12.4% of mail ballots being rejected in its first primary, versus under 1% before. Texas Tribune, Apr 2022
  • Enforcement Counterpoint: strict in-person ID laws showed no detectable effect on overall turnout, 2008–2018. The study did not look at unhoused voters or mail ballots. Cantoni & Pons, NBER w25522, 2019

Affects voting access, not housing. Scored −1, the cap for indirect effects.

Prop 316 — Municipal grocery tax cap

Statute (HCR 2021)

0.0 Mixed effect

Caps city grocery taxes at 2% and requires voter approval to raise them. Phoenix, Tucson and Mesa have no grocery tax, so the effect falls on smaller towns: lower food costs for low-income households, and less general-fund revenue for local services.

Data behind the score
  • Prevention Each 1-point rise in a grocery tax is associated with a 0.84% higher probability of food insecurity among low-income households. Zheng et al., Food Policy, 2021
  • Services & funding 70 of 91 cities and towns taxed groceries in 2024; Phoenix, Tucson and Mesa do not. Ballotpedia News, Jun 2025
  • Services & funding Rural towns rely on the tax for a large share of revenue; Bisbee warns of service cuts. Arizona Mirror, Sep 2026

No JLBC fiscal summary exists for this referral; the 2025 fiscal note scored an earlier full repeal.

Prop 320 — School district budgets

Statute (SCR 1032)

−1.0 Increases homelessness

Requires large districts to spend 60% of operating budgets on instruction or lose Classroom Site Fund money. Counselors, social workers and nurses count outside instruction, and they are the staff who identify and serve homeless students.

Housing supplyPreventionServices & fundingEnforcement
——−1record—
Data behind the score
  • Services & funding Ballot language: 60% instructional target; 25% annual loss of Classroom Site Fund money for non-compliance. Secretary of State ballot language, Aug 2026
  • Services & funding Counselors, social workers, nurses and attendance staff are classified as student support, not instruction. Arizona Auditor General, Sep 2023
  • Services & funding Statewide instructional share was 52% in FY2025; 76 districts with about 83% of students are covered. News From The States, Sep 2026
  • Services & funding About 26,840 Arizona K–12 students were identified as homeless in 2025 (op-ed citing ADE data). Arizona Capitol Times, Aug 2026

No outcome research exists on 60% mandates and homeless students; the score rests on the spending definitions.

Maricopa County

Prop 413 is the only countywide measure. Clerk of the Superior Court, community college, Valleywise board, justice of the peace and judicial retention races have no homelessness record to score.

Maricopa County Prop 413 — Jail excise tax extension

20-year extension of the 0.2-cent jail tax

Not scored

Extends the tax that funds county jail construction, operations, inmate programs and probation, about $300M a year. State law also allows the money for programs that reduce jail costs, which can include diversion.

Data behind the score
  • Enforcement Measure description. Maricopa County Elections, 2026
  • Enforcement Authorizing statute allows jail facilities and programs that reduce their expense. SB 1144 summary, 2025
  • Enforcement Raises about $300M a year; Supervisor Gallardo has cited reentry, mental health, substance use and diversion uses. KTAR, Dec 2024

Not scored. No source ties this revenue to homelessness outcomes, and the split between jail operations and diversion is not set in the measure.
