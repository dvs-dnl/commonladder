Nobody Should Have to Choose Between a Bed and Their Dog

By Common Ladder · August 18, 2026 · 4 min read

If you need somewhere to go tonight and you have an animal with you, skip this post: open the Pet-Friendly Resources Finder, answer as much or as little as you want, and it will give you the phone numbers. Everything below is why we built it.

Outreach workers in Phoenix will tell you about the conversation that ends the same way every time. Someone has been offered a bed. They want the bed. Then they ask about the dog, and the answer is no, and they go back to the wash or the alley or the car. It gets logged as a refusal of services. It is nothing of the kind. It is a person declining to hand over the one living thing that has stayed with them — often through the exact stretch of years when everything and everyone else did not.

Treating that as an obstacle to argue away is understandable and completely backwards. For a lot of unsheltered people the animal is a reason to still be here: something to feed in the morning, warmth at three a.m., a reason not to use. Asking someone to trade it for a mat on a floor is asking them to give up the load-bearing wall to get the roof fixed.

The strange part is how much already exists

Maricopa County is, quietly, better resourced for this than almost anywhere. Sojourner Center runs suite-style rooms with dog doors and a dog park next door so survivors of domestic violence and their animals shelter together — dogs, cats, birds, reptiles, fish — at no cost, in a country where only a small minority of domestic violence shelters take animals at all. New Life Center in the West Valley does the same. Lost Our Home Pet Rescue in Tempe will hold a pet for up to 90 days and hand them back. The Arizona Humane Society runs 30 to 90 days for families in crisis, plus 30 days specifically for someone entering a domestic violence shelter that cannot take the animal. Family Promise shelters families with children and any number of pets. There is a dog run on the plaza at the Key Campus downtown.

Almost none of the people who need this know it exists. Neither, sometimes, does the person answering the phone at the front desk. The information is scattered across a county web page, three rescue websites, and the institutional memory of a handful of case managers.

“Temporary care” is not surrender, and the words matter

The single most useful thing in this tool may be a piece of vocabulary. If you call a rescue and ask whether you can board your dog, you get a price. If you say you need temporary care because of a crisis, you get a free program built for exactly your situation, with the explicit purpose of returning the animal to you. Same organization, same phone number, different door.

People hear “the rescue will take your dog” and reasonably assume it means goodbye. It does not. You keep the animal, and the reunion is the entire point — it is what these organizations measure themselves on.

What the tool does

It asks what is happening — tonight, leaving an abuser, losing the housing, going into treatment or the hospital or jail, or simply not being able to afford the animal anymore — and whether you would rather stay together or have someone hold your pet for a while. Three optional taps for children, veteran status, and service animals unlock programs that not everyone can use. Nothing is required. You can press the button having answered nothing and get the whole list.

What comes back is a set of cards with a phone number, an address, who the program serves, and one line on what to actually say when someone picks up. There is also a section on what a “no” is worth: service animals are not pets, staff may ask only two questions about them, and there is no certificate you were supposed to buy. If your animal is a trained service animal, a no-pets rule is generally not supposed to apply to you at all — and being turned away anyway is worth a call to Community Legal Services rather than a night outside.

What it will not do

It will not promise you a bed. Pet space at a shelter is usually a handful of kennels, and a program with room this morning may have none tonight. Every listing is a call to make, not a guarantee. Foster programs run wait lists, and several ask for vaccination records and spay or neuter — though most will cover what your animal is missing rather than turn you away over it.

It also collects nothing. There are no text fields anywhere in it — nothing to leak. Answers stay in the browser tab and vanish when you close it, unless you tick a box to keep them on that device, which is off by default because many people using this are on a library or shelter computer.

Everything in it was checked on August 18, 2026 against the county's own directory and the providers' own pages. Programs change without telling anyone. If you call a number and it is wrong, tell us and we will fix it that week — and if you are not sure where to start, call 2-1-1 and say “I have a pet” in the first sentence.
