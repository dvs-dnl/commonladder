Evidence Brief

More Than Half of Sheltered Homeless Adults Work. The Best Job Program Skips the “Job-Ready” Test.

By Common Ladder · September 3, 2026 · 9 min read

  • When researchers linked a census of the entire 2010 US homeless population to IRS tax records, 53% of sheltered homeless adults and about 40% of unsheltered homeless adults had formal, on-the-books earnings in the same year they were observed homeless.1
  • The work doesn’t pay: in every year from 2005 to 2015, fewer than half of the adults who were sheltered-homeless in 2010 earned more than $2,000 a year, and fewer than a quarter earned more than $12,000.1
  • The best-evidenced employment help is Individual Placement and Support (IPS): rapid search for regular competitive jobs, an employment specialist, support that continues after hire, and no “readiness” prerequisites. Across randomized trials — mostly with people with severe mental illness — IPS participants were 1.63 times as likely to obtain competitive employment (95% CI 1.46–1.82).2
  • The same meta-analysis is precise about the limits: effects on quality of life, global functioning, and mental health all crossed zero. IPS reliably produces jobs. It does not, by itself, transform everything else.2
  • Only one RCT has tested IPS with people recently homeless: 90 people just housed by Housing First in Montreal. 34% found competitive work versus 22% (crude p = 0.16; adjusted OR 2.42, 95% CI 1.13–5.16). Every other employment outcome was not significantly different. The authors called IPS “a modestly useful adjunct.”3
  • The one trial to trace a housing effect — justice-involved homeless veterans in Dallas — found adding IPS raised employment, and the people who got jobs were then more likely to exit homelessness. The housing effect was real but indirect.4
  • The first RCT of IPS delivered through homelessness services is underway in England (~500 people referred, government-funded). Results are expected in late 2027. Until then, “IPS works for homelessness” is an extrapolation.5

The most durable story Americans tell about homelessness is a two-word instruction: get a job. The story assumes the job is the missing piece — that people experiencing homelessness stand outside the labor market, and that the way back in runs through a paycheck.

The data says the arrow points the other way. Most people in homeless shelters have already followed the instruction. It didn’t house them.

The stereotype has it backwards

In 2021, a University of Chicago team led by economist Bruce Meyer did something nobody had managed before: they took a census of the entire US homeless population — everyone counted as sheltered or unsheltered homeless in the 2010 Census — and linked it, person by person, to IRS tax records and administrative benefit data. Not surveys. Not self-reports from one city. The actual earnings records of the actual population.1

Fifty-three percent of sheltered homeless adults had formal labor market earnings in the same year they were observed homeless. Among the unsheltered — the people sleeping in vehicles, tents, and doorways — the figure was still about 40%. These are on-the-books, W-2-and-1099 earnings, the kind that survive an IRS match.1

What the records also show is why the work doesn’t end the homelessness. Tracking the same people across a decade, in every single year from 2005 to 2015, fewer than half of the adults who were sheltered-homeless in 2010 earned more than $2,000 a year — and fewer than a quarter cleared $12,000. That is not a wage that rents an apartment in any American metro, and it wasn’t before or after the homeless spell either. Homelessness, the authors conclude, is a symptom of long-term deprivation — a decade of it, at least — not a year of bad luck that a job offer fixes.1

So the precise question is not why won’t they work. Most do, or try to. The question is what help actually increases work — and what, exactly, more work buys.

What IPS does differently

Employment programs for people with disabilities and disadvantages have traditionally run on a readiness model: assess the person, train the person, run them through job-readiness classes or sheltered work, and place them in a real job at the end, once they’ve proven themselves. Individual Placement and Support — IPS — was developed in mental health care in the 1990s on the opposite bet.2

In IPS, an employment specialist helps the person start searching for a regular, competitive job — the person’s own choice of job — quickly, usually within weeks. Employment support is integrated with whatever treatment or services the person already receives, and it continues after hire rather than ending at placement. And there are no prerequisites. You do not have to be sober first, symptom-free first, or “job-ready” first.5

If that structure sounds familiar, it should: it is the same zero-precondition logic as Housing First, which we’ve written about before. Skip the readiness test, deliver the thing itself, wrap support around it.

What the trials found — and didn’t

IPS has one of the deepest randomized-trial bases in behavioral health. A 2019 meta-analysis of the RCT literature by Frederick and VanderWeele at Harvard found that people randomized to IPS were 1.63 times as likely to obtain any competitive employment as people in usual vocational services (95% CI 1.46–1.82). They also kept jobs longer (d = 0.55) and earned more (d = 0.48).2

The same paper is unusually honest about what IPS does not do, and it deserves quoting in numbers: the pooled effect on quality of life was d = 0.30 with a confidence interval from −0.07 to 0.67; on global functioning, d = 0.09 (−0.09 to 0.27); on mental health, d = 0.03 (−0.15 to 0.21). Every one of those intervals includes zero. IPS is a jobs machine, and the trials show exactly that: jobs. Not a general life transformation.2

Almost all of those trials enrolled people with severe mental illness — not people experiencing homelessness as such. The homeless-specific experimental record is two small trials.

The first came out of Montreal’s arm of the At Home/Chez Soi study. Ninety people who had just been housed through scattered-site Housing First were randomized to IPS or usual services. Over eight months, 34% of the IPS group found competitive employment versus 22% of controls — a difference that was not significant in the crude comparison (p = 0.16) and significant after adjustment (OR 2.42, 95% CI 1.13–5.16). No other employment outcome differed significantly. The authors’ own summary: IPS was “a modestly useful adjunct.” Not our words — theirs.3

The second is more interesting for anyone who cares about housing. At the Dallas VA, researchers randomized justice-involved homeless veterans to a job-search group program alone or the same program plus IPS. The IPS arm was significantly more likely to secure employment — including among veterans who were chronically homeless. And the veterans who secured employment were then significantly more likely to exit homelessness and secure housing. The US Department of Labor’s evidence clearinghouse rates the trial’s causal evidence high. Note the mechanics, though: the housing effect ran through employment. The program bought jobs; the jobs, for some, bought housing.4

Being precise about what this establishes

Here is what the evidence supports saying. For a person who wants to work — and surveys and tax records agree that describes much of the homeless population — IPS is the best-tested way to make employment more likely. The effect shows up in dozens of trials in adjacent populations, in one small trial of recently housed people, and in one trial of homeless veterans where it reached all the way to housing.

Here is what the evidence does not support saying: that employment programs are a homelessness solution. Do the arithmetic the tax records suggest. If a program doubles the odds of obtaining work whose observed median pay for this population runs in the low thousands of dollars a year, it has done something real and valuable for that person — income, structure, a foothold, dignity. What it has not done is close the gap between those earnings and rent, in housing markets that are the primary driver of homelessness rates in the first place.1

This is the same precision we’ve urged about cash transfers and Housing First: interventions work for what they work for. Fund employment programs because jobs are worth having and people experiencing homelessness want them — not because a jobs program will make the shelter census fall. Programs sold on outcomes they can’t deliver get defunded when they don’t deliver them.

The gap that’s about to close

Remarkably, no one has ever run a large randomized trial of IPS delivered through homelessness services themselves — shelters, housing programs, street outreach — rather than through mental-health systems. That trial is finally happening: in England, the Centre for Homelessness Impact and the UK housing ministry have randomized roughly 500 people referred from homelessness services across eight local areas, with competitive employment as the primary outcome and results expected in late 2027.5

Until those results land, every claim that “IPS works for homelessness” is an extrapolation from people with severe mental illness, plus ninety people in Montreal, plus one veterans trial in Dallas. We think the extrapolation is reasonable. It is still an extrapolation, and we’ll report what the trial finds either way.

Frequently asked questions

Do people experiencing homelessness work?

Yes, at rates that surprise most people. When researchers linked a census of the entire 2010 US homeless population to IRS tax records, 53 percent of sheltered homeless adults and about 40 percent of unsheltered homeless adults had formal, on-the-books earnings in the same year they were observed homeless. The problem is what the work pays: in every year from 2005 to 2015, fewer than half of the adults who were sheltered-homeless in 2010 earned more than $2,000 a year, and fewer than a quarter earned more than $12,000.

What is Individual Placement and Support (IPS)?

IPS is a form of supported employment developed in mental health care. Instead of assessments, training courses, and job-readiness classes before anyone touches a real job, an employment specialist helps the person search quickly for a regular competitive job matching their preferences, and support continues after hire. There are no readiness prerequisites — you do not have to be sober, symptom-free, or housed first. Across randomized trials, mostly with people with severe mental illness, IPS participants were about 1.6 times as likely to obtain competitive employment as people getting usual vocational services.

Does getting a job end homelessness?

Not by itself, in most of the evidence. The IPS meta-analysis found strong effects on jobs, tenure, and income but effects on quality of life, functioning, and mental health that crossed zero. The one homeless-specific trial that examined housing, with justice-involved homeless veterans in Dallas, found that adding supported employment raised employment, and the people who became employed were then more likely to exit homelessness — an indirect path. Meanwhile, most sheltered homeless adults already work in the formal labor market, and homelessness persists because the earnings do not cover housing.

Where can someone get free job help in Phoenix?

Our guide to free job help in Phoenix covers the current options, including ARIZONA@WORK job centers and St. Joseph the Worker, which provides interview clothes, transportation help, and job coaching for people experiencing homelessness. If you are not sure where to start, call 211. Programs change — verify hours and locations before you go.
