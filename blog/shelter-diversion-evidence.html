Evidence Brief

Maricopa Requires Diversion at Every Front Door. Its Rulebook Never Says What Diversion Is.

By Common Ladder · October 19, 2026 · 9 min read

  • Maricopa Regional CoC's Coordinated Entry Policies and Procedures, last updated by its board on June 11, 2026, require every Access Site to “Apply a diversion strategy.” The document never defines diversion, does not list it among assessment training topics, and never mentions financial assistance for it.1 The CoC's 2026 federal application, by contrast, says it trains providers on diversion, runs diversion assistance and flex funding, and has released a regionwide Prevention and Diversion Toolkit — none of which the binding rule references.9
  • The strongest causal evidence anyone cites for diversion is not about diversion. It is about cash: Chicago's prevention call center cut three-month shelter entry by 1.4 points off a 1.6% baseline (88%) and six-month shelter days by 2.6 off 3.1 (84%) — by handing out roughly one month's rent, usually under $1,000, paid to a landlord.2
  • That study is quasi-experimental, not randomized. Its comparison group exists only because some callers rang on days the money had run out.2
  • Base rates break the arithmetic. Only 2% of families denied that assistance entered shelter within six months. In New York City, only 12.8% of families who applied for prevention services entered shelter within three years.3 Most people you divert were never going to enter shelter — which is exactly why diversion looks so effective.
  • The one randomized trial of diversion itself has no results. Notre Dame's LEO has been running an RCT with Strategies to End Homelessness in Cincinnati, randomizing callers to an offer or a four-week wait. No findings, no completion date.4
  • The headline program numbers are uncontrolled: across 1,898 Puget Sound families, 49% were diverted at $1,668 average, 82.6% had not returned within a year, median 37 days to housing. No comparison group.5
  • Diversion can do harm. A three-year Canadian study of five youth programs — 30 staff and 47 young people interviewed — found inconsistent definitions, non-comparable success measures, youth logged as diverted because the shelter was full, and some sent back to unsafe homes. Its 68% diversion rate applies only to youth reached before first shelter contact.6
  • In Maricopa there is often nowhere to divert to. The family waitlist passed 200 households this summer against a stated seven-week average wait; family shelter and transitional units fell from 769 in 2024 to 630 in 2025; the state cut $867,000. The 2026 count found 2,103 people in homeless families, the most since 2015. A 2023 evaluation found 94% of those assessed did not receive housing help.7

A mother calls 211. She and her two kids have been staying with her sister for three weeks and the sister's lease says four people, not six. There is no shelter bed tonight. The worker does not say no. The worker asks questions: is there anyone else, is there a way to make the sister's place work a little longer, what would it take.

That is diversion — the busiest intervention in the homeless response system, and the least examined. Everyone at the front door does it. Almost nobody can say what it is, and nobody has shown that it works.

A requirement the rulebook never defines

Maricopa Regional CoC publishes the rules that govern every access point in the county. The current Coordinated Entry Policies and Procedures — first adopted January 2018, most recently updated by the CoC Board on June 11, 2026 — mentions diversion exactly twice. Section 3.03 requires each Access Site to “Apply a diversion strategy” so that households able to use their own resources do not enter the homeless system. Section 5.02.01 says prevention prioritization should “divert those who have housing options to that option.”1

That is the whole of it. The document never says what a diversion strategy consists of, what counts as a successful one, or how to record it. Section 5.01 lists the topics access-site staff must be trained on; diversion is not among them. No financial assistance is attached to diversion anywhere in it.1

The pieces exist elsewhere. In its 2026 consolidated application to HUD, the CoC says it instructs providers on applying diversion, operates “eviction diversion, diversion assistance, and flex funding programs,” and has released a regionwide Prevention and Diversion Toolkit for cities, towns and tribal communities.9 But the application also offers no definition, and the binding rule names none of those resources. A worker reading the rule they are held to learns that diversion is required and nothing about what it is, or what they may offer.

The best evidence is about money, not conversation

When diversion is defended with research, the research is almost always Chicago's Homelessness Prevention Call Center. Evans, Sullivan and Wallskog published it in Science in 2016, and the effect sizes are genuinely large. Shelter entry within three months fell by 1.4 percentage points against a baseline of 1.6% — an 88% reduction. Shelter days over the following six months fell by 2.6 against a baseline of 3.1 — 84%.2

Two things matter more than the headline. First, the design: nobody was randomized. Whether a caller got help depended on whether an agency had funds left that day, and the authors use that unpredictability as their source of variation. A good natural experiment, not a trial.2

Second, and more important: the intervention was money. A typical household got one month of rent paid directly to a landlord, generally under $1,000, to clear a specific financial problem.2 That is not a problem-solving conversation. It is a check. Citing this study to justify a diversion mandate that comes with no money attached inverts what it found.

Low base rates make everything look like it works

Here is the number that should discipline every diversion claim you read. Among the Chicago families who were denied prevention assistance, only 2% entered shelter within six months. In New York City, of all families who applied for prevention services, only 12.8% entered shelter within three years.3

Read that against a program reporting a 90% diversion rate. If nine in ten people walking through your door were not going to end up in shelter regardless, a program that does nothing at all posts a diversion rate in the high eighties. The measure is almost entirely base rate.

This is the central targeting problem in prevention work, and it cuts both ways: intervene early, while a crisis is cheap to solve, and you mostly reach people who would have been fine; intervene late, when risk is unmistakable, and the damage is done.3 Diversion sits at the earliest, least-targeted end of that trade.

The trial that would settle it has not reported

There is one randomized trial of diversion. Notre Dame's Wilson Sheehan Lab for Economic Opportunities is running it with Strategies to End Homelessness in Cincinnati: consenting callers are randomly assigned either to an offer of rental assistance and three to six months of case management, or to a control group not enrolled for at least four weeks.4

The project page names the researchers and the intended outcomes. It reports no results, no sample size and no completion date.4 Note what is being tested: again, rental assistance plus case management — money and staff time, not a conversation.

What the program numbers can and cannot show

The figures that circulate in diversion training decks come from program self-evaluation. The best-known set is from two Puget Sound pilots in King and Pierce Counties, published in 2018: 1,898 households with children, 49% housed quickly through diversion, $1,668 average cost, a median 37 days to housing, and 82.6% with no return to homelessness within a year.5

Useful operational numbers, not evidence of effect: there is no comparison group, so the 49% cannot be separated from the base rate above, and the 82.6% non-return figure covers only families the program housed and could still find a year later. And the $1,668 average means this version of diversion had a budget.

Where it goes wrong

The most careful look at diversion's failure modes comes from Canadian youth research: a three-year study across five shelter diversion programs, with a youth advisory committee, two universities, and interviews with 30 staff and 47 young people.6

It found that diversion meant different things at different agencies; that success was measured in non-comparable ways; that some youth were recorded as diverted only because the shelter was full that night; that some were diverted back into homes that were not safe, which the authors describe plainly as harm; and that several were diverted repeatedly. Crucially, the researchers lost touch with most youth who reached stable housing, so couch surfing, hidden homelessness and later shelter re-entry are largely missing from the outcome data.6

Their frequently quoted figure — that upwards of 68% of youth can be diverted — carries a condition that usually gets dropped: it applies to young people reached before they first contact an emergency shelter.6 The authors still call diversion promising. They also say the research needed to call it a best practice has not been done.

In Maricopa, diversion is running into an empty system

Diversion assumes it steers people away from a service they could otherwise have had. Here, that is now questionable.

ProPublica reported on October 9, 2026 that the UMOM-run family shelter waitlist passed 200 families over the summer, against an organization-stated average wait of about seven weeks. Family shelter and transitional units fell from 769 to 630 in a year as relief funds expired, while the 2026 count found 2,103 people in homeless families, the most since 2015. A 2023 evaluation of the county's coordinated entry system found that 94% of people assessed for housing help did not receive it, and that only 6% of assessed families got help with permanent housing.7

UMOM's chief executive described the practice in diversion's own language: “Let's try to figure your situation out first.”7 A 211 operator put the other side of it to a caller: “It's not me telling you no. It's the system.”7

With a bed and a deposit behind it, diversion is a genuine offer of a faster, less disruptive route. With a 200-family waitlist and no money, the same conversation is a queue-management tool wearing a best-practice label. The script does not change. What it is doing does.

What would actually settle this

Put the definition in the rule. Lift the toolkit's working definition into the policies, say what a successful diversion is and how it is recorded, and add diversion to the Section 5.01 training list. A mandate without a definition is unmeasurable by construction.

Connect the money to the door. Every study with a real effect size — Chicago, Cincinnati, Puget Sound — tested diversion with flexible funds. The CoC says flex funds exist; the access-site rule should say how much, who can authorize them, and for what.

Record the denominator. A diversion rate without a comparison group is uninterpretable. Programs that track what happens to households they could not serve — the design that made the Chicago study possible — would produce usable local evidence within a year.

None of this argues against diversion; a quick, dignified solution beats a shelter bed. The argument is narrower: diversion is carried by a requirement its own rule never defines, justified by a study of a different intervention, and measured by a statistic that mostly reflects who walked in. That is a thin foundation for the busiest decision point in the system.

Questions people ask

Is diversion the same thing as being turned away?

It is not supposed to be. Diversion should end with a safe place the household chose, and help making it work; being turned away ends with nothing. But the two are recorded identically in most systems, which is why the Canadian youth research could not always tell them apart.6

If I call 211 and get asked these questions, am I being denied shelter?

Not necessarily, and it is reasonable to ask directly. Two questions are worth asking out loud: am I on a waitlist, and if so where; and is there any financial help available to make the option we are discussing work. Marybeth Shinn, whose team produced the New York prevention research cited here, made the first point to ProPublica about Maricopa specifically: “The process should be transparent. People should know if they're on a waitlist.”7

Does Maricopa County have an eviction diversion program?

A pilot was announced on May 26, 2026, in partnership with the City of Phoenix, targeting Phoenix ZIP codes with the highest eviction filing counts, with development funded in the county's tentative FY 2027 budget. As of this writing the county has published no eligibility criteria, no start date, no program phone number and no application route, saying details “will be shared as they are available.”8 Treat it as announced, not open. For eviction help that exists today, start with free legal help in Phoenix and prevention and rental assistance.
