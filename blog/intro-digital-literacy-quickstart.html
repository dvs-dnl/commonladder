The Paper Clip Is the Barrier

By Common Ladder · September 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Short version: we built the Digital Literacy Quick-Start. Eight short walkthroughs of the online steps that benefits, housing, court, and job applications now assume you already know — getting a free email address, signing in safely on a library computer, attaching a document, photographing a paper document so it is actually accepted, joining a video call, uploading to a portal, and spotting a scam. Plus a verified list of where to get a free computer, free internet, and a low-cost device of your own in Maricopa County. No account, no test, no score, nothing stored anywhere but your own browser.

Where applications actually die

Spend any time near an intake desk and you notice the same failure over and over. It is almost never that someone cannot fill out a form. It is that the form is submitted and the proof never follows — because “upload your pay stubs” involves four separate skills nobody taught, and every one of them is a place to get stuck.

You need an email address to receive the request. You need a way to turn a piece of paper into a file. You need to know that choosing a file is not the same as uploading it, because on most portals there is a second button and it is easy to miss. And you need to do all of that inside a 30-minute session on a public computer, with a line behind you.

None of that is a literacy problem. It is a specific, learnable, roughly-two-hours problem that almost nothing in the safety net is built to teach, because the systems that require these steps assume the skill arrived with the smartphone.

Why we built it as eight separate pieces

The instinct with a topic like this is a curriculum — start at the beginning, work through to the end. We did the opposite deliberately. Somebody who needs to join a court hearing on Thursday does not need lesson one; they need the video-call section and nothing else. So each of the eight is self-contained, opens on its own, and assumes nothing from the ones before it.

There is also no test and no score. Digital skills get taught with quizzes more often than almost any other adult subject, and for an audience that has usually been assessed into and out of a lot of services already, a score is a reason to close the tab. You can tick a box to mark a section done, and that tick is only there so the page remembers where you left off — it is stored in your own browser and sent nowhere. On a library computer it disappears when the machine resets, which is exactly what you want it to do.

The parts that are specific to being unhoused

Generic tech tutorials assume a private laptop. That assumption is doing a lot of work, and removing it changes the advice.

So there is a whole section on signing in safely on a shared machine — refusing “save password,” using a private window, signing out rather than just closing the tab — because on a shared computer that habit is the difference between privacy and handing your benefits account to the next person. The email section says to write your address and password on paper and keep the paper somewhere other than your phone, since a lost phone is how most people lose the account that everything else routes through. The video-call section leads with the fact that every one of these platforms has a dial-in number, because for a court date, appearing by phone counts and not appearing does not.

The scam section is there for a blunt reason: people applying for benefits and housing get targeted specifically, because the timing is predictable and the pressure is real. Four rules cover nearly all of it. No agency asks for gift cards. Urgency is the tell. Do not click the link — go to the site yourself. Nobody legitimate needs your password, and a texted one-time code is a password.

Where the hardware comes from

Skills without a machine are not much use, so the last section is the access list, and everything in it was checked against the operator’s own site rather than a directory.

The finding most worth repeating: Maricopa County Library District lets you use a public computer with a free guest pass — you do not need a library card to get online. All 18 branches have computers, printers, and scanners; sessions may be capped at 30 or 60 minutes when others are waiting; your history and downloads are deleted at logout; and the Wi-Fi reaches the parking lot while the branch is open. With a card, which is free, you can also borrow a Chromebook and a Wi-Fi hotspot for three weeks, picked up in person with a librarian walking you through it. Phoenix Public Library’s PHXWorks pairs computer access with resume help, and branches run free basic computer classes with laptops provided.

For a machine of your own, PCs for People ships refurbished computers and $15-a-month internet to anyone enrolled in an income-based assistance program or under 200% of the federal poverty guidelines — photo ID and a dated eligibility document required, and, notably, no proof of citizenship. Computers 2 Kids sells refurbished desktops and laptops in Phoenix in the $80–$100 range. And Lifeline, the federal $9.25 monthly phone and internet discount, is still running — it is not the pandemic-era Affordable Connectivity Program, which ended, and the two get confused constantly.

One thing that is gone

Arizona had a genuinely good answer to this problem: the Connect Arizona Digital Navigator program, a statewide line you could call to get a person to walk you through a tech problem one-on-one. It was discontinued on April 1, 2025 when its federal funding through the Institute of Museum and Library Services ended. Plenty of directories still list it.

We are naming that rather than quietly routing around it, because it matters where the front door moved. Public libraries are now carrying this work, and the county system takes one-on-one Book-A-Librarian appointments at (602) 652-3000. The tool ends with a sentence you can say at any library desk to get that help, because naming the specific task — “I need to email a document to my caseworker and I have not done that before” — gets you someone who will sit down with you, rather than a flyer for a class next month.

What it does not do

It does not collect anything: no name, no email, no sign-up, no analytics on what you tick. It is not a course and gives no certificate. It will not be right about every button forever, since the apps rearrange themselves constantly — the tool says so, and says that when a step does not match, it is the software that changed and not you.

Phone numbers, prices, and program rules were verified on September 8, 2026. Call ahead before you travel, especially if the trip costs a fare you cannot spare. If you cannot reach anyone, call 2-1-1 — free, 24 hours a day, statewide — and ask for computer or internet access near you.
